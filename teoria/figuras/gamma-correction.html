Color science · display encoding

Why the pixel value
isn't the light value

Gamma correction is the fix for a mismatch between how your eyes weigh brightness and how a screen's numbers relate to the photons it emits. Here's why it exists, why it's named after a Greek letter, and what γ = 2.2 actually does to a value between 0 and 1.

01

Why it exists

Human vision is sensitive to ratios of brightness, not fixed amounts of light. Going from near-total darkness to twice as much light is obvious. Going from a bright wall to a slightly brighter one, by the same absolute amount of light, is nearly invisible. Your eye compresses a huge range of physical intensity into a much smaller range of perceived steps — and it spends most of those steps down in the shadows.

An 8-bit image channel only has 256 possible values. If those 256 codes were spaced to match equal physical light, most of them would fall in a highlight range you can barely tell apart, while the shadows — where your eye is most discerning — would get only a handful of codes, banding badly. Gamma encoding re-spaces those 256 codes to match perception instead of physics: more codes where the eye needs them, fewer where it doesn't.

02

Why it's called gamma

The relationship between an input and an output is a power function, output = input^γ, and γ (gamma) is simply the exponent's name. It isn't a fully arbitrary label: early CRT monitors produced brightness roughly proportional to voltage^2.2 — a side effect of the physics of the electron gun, not a deliberate design. Images had to be pre-distorted by the inverse power before broadcast or storage, or they'd look wrong once the CRT's own curve was applied. The number 2.2 stuck around as a standard long after CRTs disappeared, partly out of inertia and partly because it happens to land close to how human contrast sensitivity actually behaves.

05

The two curves

Encoding and decoding are inverse powers of each other. A camera or file format encodes linear light with x^(1/2.2), bowing the curve upward and lifting shadow detail into more of the available codes. A display then decodes that stored value with x^2.2, bowing it back down to recover the correct physical light. Applied in sequence, the two powers cancel out exactly.

linear  y = x
encode  y = x^(1/2.2)  — linear → stored
decode  y = x^2.2  — stored → linear light
